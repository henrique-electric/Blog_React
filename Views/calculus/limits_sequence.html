The Rigor of Limits: A Definitive Guide

From heuristic approximation to the formal Epsilon-Delta $(\epsilon-\delta)$ proof architecture.

1. The Philosophical Failure of "Infinitely Small"

For centuries, the pioneers of calculus (Newton and Leibniz) utilized a dangerous shortcut: the infinitesimal. They treated $dx$ as a quantity that was "not quite zero, but smaller than any real number." This worked wonders for calculating planetary motion, but it was logically bankrupt. If $dx$ is not zero, you cannot divide by it without error; if it is zero, the division is undefined.

2. The Topological Intuition

The modern limit defines convergence as a process of "trapping." We don't care what happens at the destination; we care that we can force the output to be arbitrarily close to the limit $L$ by staying within a sufficiently small neighborhood of the input $c$.

The Formal Quantifier Logic

Definition: The Limit

$\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ if and only if:

$$ \forall \epsilon > 0, \; \exists \delta > 0 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad 0 < |x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \epsilon $$

3. Deep-Dive: Strategy for Proofs

To master the $\epsilon-\delta$ proof, one must embrace the "Challenge-Response" mental model. The proof is effectively a negotiation between two parties: the Skeptic (who chooses $\epsilon$) and the Prover (who chooses $\delta$).

  1. The Scratchwork Phase: This is where you manipulate the inequality $|f(x) - L| < \epsilon$. Your goal is to transform this into the form $|x - c| < \text{something involving } \epsilon$.
  2. The Bounding phase: Often, the "something involving $\epsilon$" will still contain $x$. You must bound $x$ within a temporary, safe region (like $|x - c| < 1$) to find a constant numerical bound for the extra variables.
  3. The Formal Presentation: You state your choice of $\delta = \min(1, \epsilon/K)$ and logically reconstruct the proof forward, showing that the chosen $\delta$ forces the error bound $\epsilon$ to be satisfied.

4. The Squeeze Theorem and Convergence

Sometimes direct algebraic isolation is impossible. The Squeeze Theorem allows us to assert the existence of a limit even when the function itself is too complex to manipulate directly. By constructing two "bounding" functions $g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$ that share the same limit at $c$, we force $f(x)$ to conform to that same limit.

5. Discontinuities and DNE (Does Not Exist)

A limit fails to exist if the function behaves erratically as $x \to c$. Common culprits include:

  • Jump Discontinuities: The left-hand limit and right-hand limit do not agree.
  • Infinite Oscillations: Functions like $\sin(1/x)$ which whip up and down infinitely fast as $x \to 0$.
  • Unbounded Behavior: Functions that approach $\infty$ or $-\infty$.
