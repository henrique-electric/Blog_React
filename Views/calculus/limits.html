Formal Condition

The Ghost of Departed Quantities

When Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz invented calculus in the 17th century, it was a mathematical superpower. They could suddenly calculate planetary orbits, instantaneous velocities, and maximum profits. But there was a glaring, embarrassing flaw at the heart of their invention: it rested on a logical paradox.

To find the slope of a curve, they divided a tiny change in $y$ by a tiny change in $x$. They called these tiny changes "infinitesimals"—numbers that were somehow smaller than any normal number, yet strictly greater than zero. But at the very end of their calculations, they would casually set these infinitesimals exactly equal to zero to simplify the algebra.

The philosopher George Berkeley fiercely attacked this. You cannot divide by a number assuming it is not zero, and then later declare it to be zero. He mocked infinitesimals as "the ghosts of departed quantities." For nearly 150 years, calculus worked perfectly in physics and engineering, but its mathematical foundation was practically magic.

The Arrival of the Limit

In the 19th century, mathematicians like Augustin-Louis Cauchy and Karl Weierstrass finally fixed the foundation. They banned the concept of "infinitely small numbers." Instead, they introduced a dynamic process: The Limit. A limit doesn't care what happens exactly at zero. It only cares about the trend as you approach zero.

Why "Plugging It In" is a Trap

In high school algebra, if you are asked to find what a function is doing at $x = 2$, you just evaluate $f(2)$. Therefore, many students wrongly believe that $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x)$ is just a fancy way of writing $f(2)$.

This is a fundamental misunderstanding. The limit is entirely about the journey, not the destination. A limit asks: "As my inputs get arbitrarily close to 2, what value are my outputs zeroing in on?"

Consider the function $f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2}$. If you plug in $x = 2$, you get $\frac{0}{0}$, which is completely undefined. The universe breaks. The function has a literal hole in it. But if you plug in $x = 1.99$, the output is $3.99$. If you plug in $x = 2.001$, the output is $4.001$. The trend is overwhelmingly obvious: the outputs are approaching 4. The limit exists and equals 4, even though the function itself does not exist there.

The Epsilon-Delta Game: The Ultimate Proof

We know intuitively that $\lim_{x \to 2} (2x) = 4$. But "getting close to" is not rigorous mathematics. How do we prove it? We play the Epsilon-Delta game.

Imagine I am a skeptic. I don't believe your limit is exactly 4. I challenge you: "I bet you can't force the output of your function to be within 0.1 of 4." This tolerance is called Epsilon ($\epsilon$).

To win the challenge, you must give me a control knob for the input. You say, "Okay, as long as you keep your $x$ inputs within 0.05 of 2, my outputs will absolutely stay within 0.1 of 4." This input window is called Delta ($\delta$).

I shrink the challenge: "What if $\epsilon = 0.0001$?" You respond: "Then restrict $\delta$ to $0.00005$."

If you can successfully provide a valid $\delta$ for any conceivable $\epsilon$ I throw at you, no matter how microscopically small, you win the game. You have formally proven the limit.

1. The Informal Definition

We say that the limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $c$ is $L$, written as:

$$ \lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L $$

If we can make the values of $f(x)$ arbitrarily close to $L$ (as close as we like) by taking $x$ to be sufficiently close to $c$ (on either side of $c$) but not equal to $c$.

2. The Formal $\epsilon-\delta$ Definition

To eliminate words like "arbitrarily close" and "sufficiently close", Karl Weierstrass introduced the strict logical formulation. Let $f$ be a function defined on an open interval containing $c$ (except possibly at $c$ itself), and let $L$ be a real number.

The limit $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ if and only if:

$$ \forall \epsilon > 0, \; \exists \delta > 0 \quad \text{such that} \quad 0 < |x - c| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \epsilon $$

Let's dissect this mathematical sentence piece by piece:

  • $\forall \epsilon > 0$: "For every epsilon greater than zero." This is the challenge. Someone hands you a required error tolerance.
  • $\exists \delta > 0$: "There exists a delta greater than zero." This is your response. You must find an input window.
  • $0 < |x - c| < \delta$: "If the distance between $x$ and $c$ is less than $\delta$ (and $x \neq c$)." This is the assumption. We never care what happens exactly at $c$, which is why the distance is strictly greater than 0.
  • $\implies |f(x) - L| < \epsilon$: "Then the distance between the output $f(x)$ and the target $L$ is guaranteed to be less than epsilon." This is the winning condition.
The Order of Quantifiers Matters

Notice that $\forall \epsilon$ comes before $\exists \delta$. This means $\delta$ is allowed to depend on $\epsilon$ (we often write $\delta(\epsilon)$). You do not need to find one magical $\delta$ that works for every $\epsilon$. You just need a rule that generates a new $\delta$ whenever a new $\epsilon$ is provided.

3. Left and Right Limits

Sometimes a function behaves completely differently depending on which direction you approach from. We use directional limits to handle this.

  • Right-hand limit ($x \to c^+$): We only consider $x$ values strictly greater than $c$. The formal condition becomes $0 < x - c < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \epsilon$.
  • Left-hand limit ($x \to c^-$): We only consider $x$ values strictly less than $c$. The formal condition becomes $0 < c - x < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \epsilon$.

The Ultimate Theorem: A general limit $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ exists if and only if both the left-hand limit and the right-hand limit exist, and they equal the exact same number $L$. If a function jumps vertically, the left and right limits disagree, and the general limit Does Not Exist (DNE).

4. The Squeeze Theorem

If you cannot evaluate a limit directly, you can trap it between two functions that are easier to evaluate. If $g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$ for all $x$ near $c$, and if:

$$ \lim_{x \to c} g(x) = \lim_{x \to c} h(x) = L $$

Then the function $f(x)$ is forced (squeezed) to have the exact same limit: $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$. This is historically how we prove that $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$, by trapping it between $\cos x$ and $1$.

Proof 1: A Linear Function

Formally prove that $\lim_{x \to 2} (3x - 1) = 5$ using the Epsilon-Delta definition.

1

State what must be shown

We must show that for any given $\epsilon > 0$, we can find a $\delta > 0$ such that:

$$ 0 < |x - 2| < \delta \implies |(3x - 1) - 5| < \epsilon $$
2

Phase 1: The "Scratchwork"

We start with the conclusion we want to reach and work backwards to find a relationship between $\delta$ and $\epsilon$. Start by simplifying the right side.

$$ |3x - 1 - 5| < \epsilon $$
$$ |3x - 6| < \epsilon $$
3

Factor out the constant

Factor out the 3 from the absolute value. This reveals the $|x - c|$ term we are looking for.

$$ 3|x - 2| < \epsilon $$
4

Solve for the delta term

Divide both sides by 3. This tells us exactly how we need to choose our $\delta$ based on the given $\epsilon$.

$$ |x - 2| < \frac{\epsilon}{3} $$
Discovery: We should choose $\delta = \frac{\epsilon}{3}$. If someone demands an error tolerance of $0.03$, we restrict the inputs to $0.01$.
5

Phase 2: The Formal Proof

Now we write the actual proof forwards. Let $\epsilon > 0$ be given. We choose $\delta = \frac{\epsilon}{3}$. Assume $0 < |x - 2| < \delta$.

Then, by our assumption:

$$ |x - 2| < \frac{\epsilon}{3} $$
6

Reconstruct the function

Multiply both sides by 3 to rebuild the original expression.

$$ 3|x - 2| < \epsilon $$
$$ |3x - 6| < \epsilon $$
$$ |(3x - 1) - 5| < \epsilon $$
Q.E.D. We have shown that choosing $\delta = \frac{\epsilon}{3}$ guarantees the function stays within $\epsilon$ of the limit 5.

Proof 2: A Non-Linear Function (Quadratic)

Formally prove that $\lim_{x \to 3} x^2 = 9$.

1

The Scratchwork

We want $|x^2 - 9| < \epsilon$. We factor this to expose the $|x - 3|$ term.

$$ |x - 3| \cdot |x + 3| < \epsilon $$
2

The Bounding Problem

Unlike the linear example, we have a rogue variable term: $|x + 3|$. We cannot set $\delta = \frac{\epsilon}{|x+3|}$ because $\delta$ must be a constant that only depends on $\epsilon$, not on $x$. We must force a numerical bound on $|x + 3|$.

3

Assume a provisional Delta

Let's pre-emptively agree that we will never let $\delta$ be larger than 1. So, assume $|x - 3| < 1$. This implies:

$$ 2 < x < 4 $$
4

Bound the rogue term

If $x$ is trapped between 2 and 4, then $(x + 3)$ is trapped between 5 and 7. Therefore, the absolute maximum value $|x + 3|$ can take is 7.

$$ |x + 3| < 7 $$
5

Finalize the Delta choice

Returning to our scratchwork, we substitute the worst-case bound: $7 |x - 3| < \epsilon$, which implies $|x - 3| < \frac{\epsilon}{7}$. However, this only works if our earlier assumption ($|x-3| < 1$) is also true.

Discovery: We must choose $\delta = \min(1, \frac{\epsilon}{7})$. We take whichever constraint is stricter.

The Architecture of a Limit Proof

Writing an $\epsilon-\delta$ proof feels backwards because it requires two completely separate phases: a private discovery phase, and a public presentation phase.

Phase 1: The Private Scratchwork
This is the messy algebraic phase you do on scrap paper. You never hand this in to a professor.
  • Start with the end goal: $|f(x) - L| < \epsilon$.
  • Use algebraic manipulation (factoring, rationalizing, multiplying by conjugates) to isolate the term $|x - c|$.
  • For linear functions, you will end up with $K|x - c| < \epsilon$, leading directly to $\delta = \epsilon/K$.
  • For non-linear functions, you will have a rogue function of $x$ leftover. You must artificially bound this rogue function by assuming a maximum radius (usually $\delta \le 1$) to find its maximum possible value $M$.
Phase 2: The Public Proof
This is the formal essay you write down. It proceeds purely forward using logical deduction.
  • Start strictly with: "Let $\epsilon > 0$ be given." You are acknowledging the challenge.
  • Declare your weapon: "Choose $\delta = [insert your scratchwork finding here]." If you used a bounding assumption, write "Choose $\delta = \min(1, \dots)$".
  • State the premise: "Assume $0 < |x - c| < \delta$."
  • Execute the logic: Reconstruct the algebra from your scratchwork in reverse order, step-by-step, arriving exactly at $|f(x) - L| < \epsilon$.
  • Conclude: "Therefore, by the definition of a limit, $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$."
When Limits Fail (Proving DNE)
To prove a limit Does Not Exist, you must break the $\epsilon-\delta$ definition. You do this by reversing the quantifiers.

Instead of $\forall \epsilon \exists \delta$, you must prove $\exists \epsilon \forall \delta$.

You only need to find one specific $\epsilon$ (usually $\epsilon = 1/2$ or the size of a jump discontinuity) where, no matter how small someone makes $\delta$, the function always manages to escape the $\epsilon$-bounds. If you can trap them, the limit cannot exist.

Glossary of Terms

Limit
The foundational concept of calculus. The value that a function "approaches" as its input approaches a specific target value, regardless of what happens exactly at that target value.
Epsilon ($\epsilon$)
A Greek letter used universally in mathematics to represent a small, strictly positive error tolerance on the output (y-axis). It defines the vertical target window $[L - \epsilon, L + \epsilon]$.
Delta ($\delta$)
A Greek letter used to represent a small, strictly positive control window on the input (x-axis). It defines the horizontal bounds $[c - \delta, c + \delta]$ needed to guarantee the epsilon tolerance.
Continuous Function
A function where the limit at every point exists, and the limit perfectly equals the actual value of the function. Formally: $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)$. There are no holes, jumps, or vertical asymptotes.
Jump Discontinuity
A point where the left-hand limit and the right-hand limit both exist, but are completely different numbers. The function jumps from one height to another. The general limit Does Not Exist (DNE) here.
Squeeze Theorem (Sandwich Theorem)
A theorem stating that if a function $f(x)$ is permanently trapped between two other functions $g(x)$ and $h(x)$, and both of those bounding functions approach the same limit $L$, then $f(x)$ must also approach $L$.
