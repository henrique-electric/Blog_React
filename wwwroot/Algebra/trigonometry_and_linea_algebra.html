§ 01 — The Foundation

Escaping the Triangle

We are often first taught trigonometry through the narrow lens of right-angled triangles. SOH-CAH-TOA is drilled into our minds as a ratio of static side lengths. But this perspective is brittle; it shatters the moment an angle exceeds 90 degrees. To truly understand trigonometry, we must completely divorce it from triangles and embed it into the heart of Linear Algebra.

Let us begin in a 2D vector space. Every point in this space can be reached by a linear combination of two fundamental, orthogonal building blocks: the basis vectors iˆ (pointing right) and jˆ (pointing up).

Sine and Cosine are not just ratios. They are the coordinates of the basis vector iˆ after it has been continuously transformed by rotation.

Imagine grasping the vector iˆ and rotating it counter-clockwise around the origin by an angle θ. As it sweeps out a unit circle, its new x-coordinate is, by absolute definition, cos(θ). Its new y-coordinate is sin(θ).

Let basis vector iˆ = [ 1, 0 ]T

After rotation by angle θ:
iˆrotated = [ cos(θ), sin(θ) ]T

We haven't computed any ratios. We have simply defined a dynamic mapping of space. Sine and Cosine are the continuous coordinate trackers of rotated basis vectors.

§ 02 — Building the Machinery

Deducing the Rotation Matrix

A central tenet of linear algebra states that to know how an entire vector space transforms, you only need to know where the basis vectors land. If we can track where iˆ and jˆ go under a rotation of angle θ, we can build a machine—a matrix—that rotates any vector in existence.

Let's deduce this slowly from scratch. We already know that rotating iˆ = [1, 0] by θ lands it at [cos θ, sin θ]. This becomes the first column of our matrix.

But what about jˆ = [0, 1]? It starts pointing straight up. If we rotate it by θ, it leans into the negative x-territory. By geometric symmetry (rotating the first result by 90°), its new x-coordinate becomes -sin θ, and its new y-coordinate becomes cos θ. This forms our second column.

// The 2D Rotation Matrix R(θ)
R(θ) = [ Transformed iˆ | Transformed jˆ ]

R(θ) = ⎡ cos θ   -sin θ ⎤
       ⎣ sin θ    cos θ ⎦

This single elegant grid of four trigonometric functions encodes the complete geometric act of rotation. If you multiply any vector [x, y]T by this matrix, it computes the exact coordinates of that vector rotated by θ.

§ 03 — A Slow-Motion Proof

Dissolving Algebraic Tedium

In high school, students are often forced to memorize the terrifying angle addition formulas:
sin(α + β) = sinα cosβ + cosα sinβ. The standard algebraic derivations are incredibly tedious and devoid of intuition. Let's use our new linear algebra machinery to deduce this formula from scratch, visually and geometrically.

What does it mean to rotate a vector by an angle α, and then subsequently rotate it by an angle β? Geometrically, it is identical to performing a single, continuous rotation by the combined angle (α + β).

In linear algebra, applying consecutive transformations is perfectly equivalent to matrix multiplication. Therefore, if we multiply the rotation matrix for β by the rotation matrix for α, the resulting matrix must be the rotation matrix for (α + β).

R(α + β) = R(β) · R(α)

⎡ cos(α+β) -sin(α+β) ⎤ = ⎡ cosβ -sinβ ⎤ · ⎡ cosα -sinα ⎤
⎣ sin(α+β)   cos(α+β) ⎦   ⎣ sinβ   cosβ ⎦   ⎣ sinα   cosα ⎦

If we perform the matrix multiplication on the right-hand side using the standard row-by-column dot product, a miracle occurs. The entries of the resulting matrix automatically yield the angle addition formulas!

Looking at the top-left entry: cos(α+β) = cosβ cosα - sinβ sinα.
Looking at the bottom-left entry: sin(α+β) = sinβ cosα + cosβ sinα.

No triangles. No complicated geometry. Just the pure mechanics of combining linear transformations.

§ 04 — Measuring Alignment

The Dot Product & Cosine

How do we extract the angle between two arbitrary vectors sitting in space? We generalize the concept of Cosine. If Cosine tracks the x-coordinate of a unit vector, it is fundamentally tracking how much of that vector points in the horizontal direction. It is a measure of alignment.

The Dot Product (or Inner Product) is the ultimate mathematical tool for measuring alignment. Geometrically, the dot product of two vectors u and v is defined as the length of u projected onto v, multiplied by the length of v.

Geometric Definition:
u · v = ||u|| · ||v|| · cos(θ)

Algebraic Definition:
u · v = (ux · vx) + (uy · vy)

By equating these two definitions, we unlock a massive computational shortcut. We can find the exact Cosine of the angle between two vectors using only basic arithmetic on their coordinates, completely bypassing the need for a protractor or inverse trig functions.

If the dot product is 0, the vectors share zero alignment; they are perfectly perpendicular (orthogonal), and cos(90°) = 0. If the dot product is negative, they are actively opposing each other.

§ 05 — Measuring Independence

The Determinant & Sine

If Cosine and the Dot Product measure alignment, then Sine measures perpendicularity, or linear independence. How much does a vector deviate from another? In a 2D space, this deviation sweeps out an area.

If we arrange two vectors u and v as columns in a 2x2 matrix, the Determinant of that matrix precisely calculates the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by those two vectors. This area is intrinsically tied to the Sine of the angle between them.

Let matrix A = [ u | v ] = ⎡ ux vx ⎤
                        ⎣ uy vy ⎦

det(A) = (ux · vy) - (uy · vx)

Geometric Meaning:
|det(A)| = ||u|| · ||v|| · |sin(θ)| = Area

Notice the symmetry. The algebraic dot product contains a plus sign and yields Cosine. The algebraic determinant contains a minus sign and yields Sine (often acting as the 2D equivalent of the Cross Product). Together, they capture the complete geometric relationship between any two vectors.

If det(A) = 0, the vectors span zero area. They are parallel or perfectly anti-parallel. sin(0°) = 0. The vectors are linearly dependent, and the matrix collapses space into a 1D line.

§ 06 — The Deep Connection

Trigonometry is Vector Geometry

We began by freeing Sine and Cosine from the rigid confines of right-angled triangles. By defining them as the coordinates of continuously rotating basis vectors, we constructed the rotation matrix—a machine capable of transforming all of space.

By shifting to matrices, tedious algebraic identities like angle addition revealed themselves as simple, intuitive consequences of geometric composition. Finally, we generalized Cosine and Sine into the Dot Product and Determinant, creating algebraic tools capable of measuring alignment and area in any dimension, without ever calculating an explicit angle.

Trigonometry is not a separate discipline from Linear Algebra. It is simply the study of how vector spaces bend and rotate, viewed through a specific numerical lens.

Summary: The Link at a Glance

Sine & Cosine

Not static ratios, but the fundamental coordinates of standard basis vectors under the action of continuous rotation.

Angle Addition Formulas

The algebraic result of multiplying two rotation matrices together. Sequential rotation equals matrix multiplication.

The Dot Product

The multi-dimensional generalization of Cosine. An algebraic operation that computes purely geometric alignment.

The Determinant

The generalization of Sine. Computes the geometric area spanned by vectors, representing their linear independence.
