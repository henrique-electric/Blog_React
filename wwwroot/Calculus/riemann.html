The Core Problem: Curved Boundaries

Geometry has always known how to measure rectangles, triangles, and polygons. A rectangle with base b and height h has area bh — this requires no calculus, no limits, no special machinery. The ancient Greeks could compute the area of any polygon by cutting it into triangles.

But what about the region beneath a curve? A parabola has a curved boundary. No finite collection of straight-edged shapes fits it exactly. This is not a computational inconvenience — it is a structural problem. The area is perfectly well-defined (the region is clearly bounded), but our geometric vocabulary does not contain the tools to name it directly.

The Riemann sum is the answer to this structural problem. Its strategy is profound in its simplicity: replace the curved boundary with a staircase of flat edges, measure the staircase exactly (since each step is a rectangle), then take the staircase to infinity — making the steps infinitely thin until the approximation becomes exact.

The Core Strategy

We are not computing the area by some formula — we are defining what it means for a curved region to have an area. The Riemann sum is simultaneously the approximation method and the foundational definition.

Why Rectangles? The Case for Simplicity

One might ask: why rectangles specifically? Why not trapezoids, or curved arches, or something that better follows the function's shape? The answer has two parts.

First, rectangles are the simplest shape whose area is beyond doubt. No calculus is needed to verify that a rectangle of width Δx and height f(x*) has area f(x*) · Δx. This is intentional: we are trying to define area for complicated shapes using the unambiguous area of simple shapes. If we used trapezoids, we would need their area formula — which itself requires some geometric argument. Rectangles are epistemically clean.

Second, and more importantly: it doesn't matter in the limit. As the partition gets finer (Δx → 0), the difference between any two reasonable methods of approximating each strip vanishes. The rectangle approximation, the trapezoid approximation, and even a curved arch approximation all converge to the same value — the true integral. So we choose the simplest.

Historical note: Archimedes used the "method of exhaustion" — bounding a region between inscribed and circumscribed polygons — long before Riemann. His calculation of the area of a parabolic segment (around 250 BCE) used exactly this squeezing argument, making him arguably the first to perform what we now call integration.

The Squeeze Theorem Argument: Why the Limit Exists

Here is the deepest structural insight: how do we know the limit of Riemann sums actually converges? The limit might not exist — different sequences of partitions might give different values. For arbitrary functions, this does happen. But for nice functions (continuous, or monotone), it doesn't. Here is why.

Define the upper sum U(f, P) as the sum where each rectangle's height is the maximum of f on that subinterval. Define the lower sum L(f, P) as the sum using the minimum. Then for any choice of sample points xi*:

L(f, P) ≤ S(f, P) ≤ U(f, P)

This is a sandwich. The Riemann sum is always trapped between the lower and upper Darboux sums. Now: what happens as the partition is refined? The lower sums increase (more rectangles means better coverage from below), and the upper sums decrease (better coverage from above). If — and this is Riemann's criterion — the gap U − L can be made arbitrarily small by choosing a fine enough partition, then the Riemann sum has no choice but to converge. The squeeze forces it.

Riemann's Criterion for Integrability

A bounded function f is Riemann-integrable on [a, b] if and only if for every ε > 0, there exists a partition P such that U(f, P) − L(f, P) < ε. This is equivalent to requiring that f has "measure zero" discontinuities — a condition satisfied by all continuous functions and all monotone functions.

When does the squeeze fail?

The squeeze fails when U − L cannot be forced below some threshold, no matter how fine the partition. The classic example is the Dirichlet function: f(x) = 1 if x is rational, f(x) = 0 if x is irrational. On any subinterval, no matter how tiny, there are both rationals and irrationals. So the maximum is always 1 and the minimum is always 0. Every upper sum equals (b − a) · 1, every lower sum equals (b − a) · 0 — the gap never closes. This function is not Riemann-integrable.

Not All Functions Are Integrable

Riemann integrability is not automatic. It requires that the function's discontinuities be "negligible" — technically, a set of measure zero. A function that is discontinuous everywhere (like Dirichlet's) cannot be integrated in Riemann's sense. The Lebesgue integral, developed in the early 1900s, handles such functions by redefining what "area" means at a deeper level.

What Δx Really Means: A Philosophical Aside

The symbol Δx (pronounced "delta x") is the width of one subinterval. In the Riemann sum, it is a finite positive number — say, 0.75 or 0.001. There is nothing infinitely small about it; it is just a small real number.

But when we write the definite integral as ∫ f(x) dx, the dx is the symbol that Δx "becomes in the limit." This is a notational convention with deep historical roots. Leibniz, who invented this notation in the 17th century, genuinely thought of dx as an infinitesimally thin rectangle — a number smaller than any positive real number yet greater than zero. This concept was philosophically controversial for 200 years.

Riemann's contribution (1854) was to make the definition rigorous without needing infinitesimals. His approach uses ordinary limits: Δx is always a real number, and we ask what happens as that real number approaches — but never reaches — zero. The notation ∫ f(x) dx preserves Leibniz's intuition while resting on Riemann's rigorous foundation.

Intuition
The staircase that becomes a slope

Imagine descending a staircase with 4 steps versus 1000 steps versus infinitely many. At infinite steps, the staircase is the ramp — the distinction collapses. Riemann sums work the same way: at the limit, the approximation is the area.

Analogy
Pixels and images

A digital image is a grid of rectangular pixels. At low resolution, you see blocky rectangles. At high enough resolution, your eye perceives a smooth curve. The "true" image is the limit as pixel size → 0. The Riemann sum is exactly the pixel approximation.

Physics
Accumulating a continuous quantity

If your speed varies continuously as v(t), your total distance is ∫ v(t) dt. You approximate it by assuming constant speed over each small time interval Δt — exactly a Riemann sum. GPS systems compute something precisely analogous thousands of times per second.

Probability
Expected value as an integral

The expected value of a continuous random variable X with density f is E[X] = ∫ x f(x) dx. This integral is a Riemann sum in disguise: weight each possible value x by its probability density f(x) and sum over all outcomes.

The Accumulation Perspective

There is a second way to understand the Riemann sum — not as approximating area, but as accumulating a rate. This perspective is arguably more powerful, and it is the one that connects most naturally to physics and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

Suppose f(x) represents a rate of change at position x — say, the density of a bar of metal at position x along its length, or the instantaneous velocity of a moving object at time x. The Riemann sum Sn = Σ f(xi*) · Δx then reads as: approximate the total accumulated quantity by assuming the rate is constant (equal to f(xi*)) over each small interval of length Δx.

In each subinterval, you are saying: "for this Δx-wide chunk, the rate held steady at f(xi*), so the amount accumulated is f(xi*) · Δx." Sum these chunks and you get the total accumulation. As Δx → 0, the approximation of "constant rate on each chunk" becomes arbitrarily precise.

Area vs. Accumulation — Two Faces of the Same Idea

Every Riemann integral is both an area (under the graph of f) and an accumulation (of the quantity whose rate is f). The area interpretation is geometric; the accumulation interpretation is physical. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus bridges the two: if F′(x) = f(x), then the total accumulated change in F from a to b equals the integral of its rate f.

Why the Midpoint Rule Is Better: A Geometric Explanation

For smooth functions, the midpoint rule achieves second-order accuracy (error ∼ 1/n²), while left and right rules achieve only first-order (error ∼ 1/n). This has a beautiful geometric explanation.

Consider a single subinterval [x₀, x₁] of width Δx. For the left rule, the rectangle has height f(x₀). For a concave-up curve, this underestimates because the curve rises above f(x₀) across the whole interval. The error on that single rectangle is roughly f′(x₀) · Δx/2 · Δx ∼ O(Δx²).

The midpoint rule, however, enjoys a special cancellation. The rectangle of height f(x̄) (where x̄ is the midpoint) overestimates on the left half of the subinterval and underestimates on the right half — the errors have opposite sign and largely cancel. More precisely, the midpoint rectangle has the same area as the tangent trapezoid at x̄, and the error between a tangent line and the curve is governed by the second derivative: it is O(Δx³) per subinterval rather than O(Δx²). Summing over n ∼ 1/Δx subintervals gives total error O(Δx²) = O(1/n²). That is the source of the quadratic convergence.

Simpson's Rule takes this further: it fits a parabola (rather than a line) through three points on each subinterval. The error per subinterval becomes O(Δx⁵), giving total error O(Δx⁴) = O(1/n⁴). Each doubling of n reduces the error by a factor of 16. This is why Simpson's Rule is the workhorse of numerical integration in practice.

The Limit in Slow-Motion: Why the Error Vanishes

When learning integration, it is tempting to memorize that "as n → ∞, the error goes to zero." But let's look at this in slow-motion to understand the geometric reality of why the error vanishes faster than the rectangles themselves shrink.

Imagine a single subinterval of width Δx. The true area under a smooth curve here is roughly a trapezoid. If we use a left Riemann sum, we are missing a small triangular wedge of area at the top. The base of this missing wedge is Δx. Its height is the difference in the function's value across the interval, which is roughly f′(x) · Δx.

Therefore, the error in one single rectangle is the area of that triangle: 1/2 · base · height ≈ 1/2 (Δx)(f′(x) Δx) = C · Δx².

The Geometry of Convergence

Notice the power of Δx². If you cut the width of the rectangles in half, the error for a single rectangle doesn't just halve—it drops by a factor of four. Because you now have twice as many rectangles, the total error over the whole interval drops by a factor of two. The error is being crushed geometrically because the "missing wedges" are shrinking in both width and height simultaneously.

Stretching Space: A Bridge to Higher Dimensions

In 1D calculus, Δx is a rigid, uniform width. But the true power of the Riemann sum reveals itself when we move into multivariable spaces, where we calculate volumes using double integrals: ∬ f(x,y) dA.

Here, the 1D line segment Δx becomes a 2D patch of area ΔA. But what if we change our coordinate system—say, moving from a rigid Cartesian grid (x, y) to a circular Polar grid (r, θ)? The "rectangles" of our partition are no longer uniform squares; they warp and stretch depending on how far they are from the origin.

The 1D Riemann sum prepares us for this. The Δx is essentially a scaling factor for the geometry of the input space. When space warps in higher dimensions, that scaling factor is replaced by the Jacobian determinant—a multiplier that tracks exactly how much a tiny localized "rectangle" of space stretches or squishes during a transformation. The logic remains identical: assume the rate f is constant over a tiny patch, multiply by the size of that patch, and accumulate.

The Bridge to the Fundamental Theorem

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC) says: if F is any antiderivative of f (meaning F′(x) = f(x)), then

∫ab f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)

This is a genuinely striking result. The integral — defined as a limit of sums on the left — turns out to equal a simple subtraction on the right. Why?

The argument is essentially a telescoping sum. Define A(x) as the "area accumulation function" — the area under f from a to x. By the definition of the integral as a Riemann sum, A(a) = 0 and A(b) = ∫ab f. Now, the key observation: A′(x) = f(x). This is the content of FTC Part 1 — the rate of change of accumulated area at position x is exactly f(x). Therefore A is an antiderivative of f, so A(x) = F(x) + C for any antiderivative F. Plugging in: A(b) − A(a) = F(b) − F(a). Done.

The Riemann sum is the scaffolding that makes this argument possible. Without the precise definition of the integral as a limit, the FTC would be a plausible heuristic, not a theorem. Riemann's definition transforms it into something provable.

The Deepest Structural Point

Integration and differentiation are inverse operations — not by coincidence, but because of how integration is defined. The Riemann sum encodes the idea of accumulation; differentiation measures rates of change; and the FTC says these two operations undo each other. Every time you use ∫ab f dx = F(b) − F(a), you are implicitly standing on Riemann's foundational definition.

Non-Uniform Partitions and the Mesh

Everything above assumed a uniform partition — all n subintervals have the same width Δx = (b−a)/n. This is the natural choice for computation, but the theory works far more generally.

A general partition P = {x₀, x₁, …, xn} can have subintervals of different widths Δxi = xi − xi−1. The Riemann sum becomes Σ f(xi*) · Δxi. This generality is important: when a function varies rapidly in one region and slowly in another, a non-uniform partition can concentrate refinement where it is needed, achieving high accuracy with fewer rectangles.

For convergence, what matters is not n → ∞ per se, but that the mesh ‖P‖ = max Δxi → 0. (For uniform partitions, these are equivalent, since ‖P‖ = Δx = (b−a)/n.) A Riemann sum over non-uniform partitions converges to the integral as ‖P‖ → 0, for any choice of sample points xi*, as long as f is Riemann-integrable.

What is a Riemann Sum?

A Riemann sum is a method for approximating the total area under a curve by dividing it into a finite number of vertical strips — each approximated as a rectangle. The central idea is elegantly simple: if we cannot compute an area exactly, we can estimate it by stacking rectangles, then make the estimate arbitrarily precise by using more and narrower rectangles.

The concept is named after the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann (1826–1866), who formalized it as the foundation of integral calculus. The Riemann sum is not merely a computational trick — it is the definition of the definite integral itself.

Formal Definition

Let f be a bounded function on [a, b]. A partition P = {x0, x1, …, xn} divides [a, b] into n subintervals. Choosing a sample point xi* ∈ [xi−1, xi] in each subinterval, the Riemann sum is:

Sn = Σi=1n f(xi*) · Δxi

where Δxi = xi − xi−1 is the width of the i-th subinterval. For a uniform partition (equal widths), Δx = (b − a) / n for all i.

The Three Standard Rules

The choice of sample point xi* within each subinterval defines which rule is used:

RuleSample PointFormulaBehavior
Left Riemann Left endpoint: xi−1 S = Σ f(xi−1) · Δx Overestimates increasing f; underestimates decreasing f
Right Riemann Right endpoint: xi S = Σ f(xi) · Δx Underestimates increasing f; overestimates decreasing f
Midpoint Rule Midpoint: (xi−1 + xi)/2 S = Σ f(x̄i) · Δx Often most accurate; error is O(Δx²)
Upper Sum Maximum of f on [xi−1, xi] U = Σ sup f · Δx Always an overestimate (or equal)
Lower Sum Minimum of f on [xi−1, xi] L = Σ inf f · Δx Always an underestimate (or equal)

Connection to the Definite Integral

The Riemann sum's deepest significance is that the definite integral is defined as its limit. As n → ∞ (and thus Δx → 0), the Riemann sum approaches the exact area — provided f is Riemann-integrable on [a, b].

∫ab f(x) dx = limn→∞ Σi=1n f(xi*) · Δx

This limit exists and equals the same value regardless of which sample points xi* are chosen — as long as f is bounded and continuous almost everywhere (Riemann's criterion).

Key insight: The integral is not computed by anti-differentiation in this framework — it is defined as this limit. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus then provides a computational shortcut via antiderivatives.

Why Negative Areas?

When f(x) < 0, the "height" f(xi*) is negative, so the rectangle contributes a negative value to the sum. This is intentional: the definite integral counts area below the x-axis as negative. The total is thus the signed area — positive regions minus negative ones.

Upper and Lower Darboux Sums

A related (and equivalent) approach uses Darboux sums: the upper sum U(f, P) uses the supremum of f on each subinterval, and the lower sum L(f, P) uses the infimum. A function is Riemann-integrable if and only if:

lim‖P‖→0 [ U(f, P) − L(f, P) ] = 0

where ‖P‖ denotes the mesh (width of the widest subinterval).

A worked example: approximate ∫₀³ x² dx using n = 4 left-endpoint rectangles.

1

Identify the function and interval

We wish to approximate the area under f(x) = x² on the interval [a, b] = [0, 3]. The exact value (by antiderivative) is x³/3, giving:

∫03 x² dx = [x³/3]03 = 27/3 − 0 = 9

We'll approximate this with rectangles and see how close we get.

2

Choose the number of subintervals n

Let n = 4. We divide [0, 3] into 4 equal pieces. The width of each piece (subinterval) is:

Δx = (b − a) / n = (3 − 0) / 4 = 0.75
Δx is the base of every rectangle. All rectangles have the same base for a uniform partition.
3

List the partition points

The endpoints of each subinterval are:

x0 = 0,  x1 = 0.75,  x2 = 1.5,  x3 = 2.25,  x4 = 3

In general: xi = a + i · Δx = 0 + i · 0.75

4

Choose sample points xi*

Using the left endpoint rule, the sample point in each subinterval [xi−1, xi] is its left endpoint xi−1:

x1* = 0,  x2* = 0.75,  x3* = 1.5,  x4* = 2.25
The sample point determines the height of each rectangle. It can be any point in the subinterval — you have freedom of choice, and any valid choice gives the same limit as n → ∞.
5

Evaluate f at each sample point

Plug each xi* into f(x) = x² to get the rectangle heights:

f(0) = 0,  f(0.75) = 0.5625,  f(1.5) = 2.25,  f(2.25) = 5.0625

These are the heights of the four rectangles.

6

Compute each rectangle's area

Area of each rectangle = height × width = f(xi*) · Δx:

0 × 0.75 = 0
0.5625 × 0.75 = 0.421875
2.25 × 0.75 = 1.6875
5.0625 × 0.75 = 3.796875
7

Sum all rectangle areas

The Riemann sum S₄ is the total of all rectangle areas:

S4 = 0 + 0.421875 + 1.6875 + 3.796875 = 5.90625
Exact value: 9  |  Approximation: 5.906  |  Error: 3.094 (34.4%)

This large error is expected with only 4 rectangles and left endpoints on an increasing function. The left rule systematically underestimates here because f is increasing — each rectangle's height is less than the function's maximum on that interval.
8

Refine by increasing n

Repeat with n = 20, 100, 1000. As n → ∞, Sn → 9:

n=4: S = 5.906 (error 34%)
n=20: S = 8.099 (error 10%)
n=100: S = 8.820 (error 2%)
n=1000: S = 8.982 (error 0.2%)

Each doubling of n roughly halves the error for the left/right rules — error ∼ O(1/n). The midpoint rule achieves O(1/n²).

Error Rates Explained

Left/Right rules: Error ≈ C · (b−a)² · f′(avg) / n. Halving Δx halves the error — first order convergence.

Midpoint rule: Error ≈ C · (b−a)³ · f″(avg) / n². Halving Δx quarters the error — second order convergence. This is why midpoint is almost always preferable.

Trapezoid rule (average of left+right): Also second order. Simpson's Rule (combining midpoint and trapezoid) achieves fourth order.

Glossary of Terms

Partition P
A finite set of points a = x0 < x1 < ⋯ < xn = b that subdivides [a, b] into n subintervals. A uniform partition has all subintervals of equal width Δx = (b−a)/n.
Subinterval [xᵢ₋₁, xᵢ]
The i-th piece of the partition. Each rectangle in the Riemann sum occupies exactly one subinterval as its base. There are n subintervals in total.
Δx (Delta x)
The width of each subinterval. For a uniform partition: Δx = (b − a) / n. As n → ∞, Δx → 0. This is simultaneously the base of each rectangle and the "step size" in the approximation.
Sample Point xᵢ*
Any point chosen from the i-th subinterval [xᵢ₋₁, xᵢ]. Its function value f(xᵢ*) becomes the height of the i-th rectangle. The choice of sample point defines the rule: left, right, midpoint, maximum, minimum, or arbitrary.
Rectangle Area
The contribution of the i-th term to the sum: f(xᵢ*) · Δx. If f(xᵢ*) < 0, this contributes a negative amount, representing area below the x-axis.
Mesh ‖P‖
The width of the widest subinterval in the partition. For non-uniform partitions, the Riemann sum converges to the integral as ‖P‖ → 0, not just as n → ∞.
Upper Sum U(f, P)
A Riemann sum where each sample point is chosen to maximize f on its subinterval: U = Σ Mi · Δx, where Mi = sup f on [xᵢ₋₁, xᵢ]. Always ≥ the true integral.
Lower Sum L(f, P)
A Riemann sum where each sample point minimizes f: L = Σ mᵢ · Δx, where mᵢ = inf f on [xᵢ₋₁, xᵢ]. Always ≤ the true integral.
Riemann-integrable
A bounded function f is Riemann-integrable on [a, b] if and only if lim‖P‖→0 [U(f,P) − L(f,P)] = 0. All continuous functions and all monotone functions on [a, b] are Riemann-integrable.
Definite Integral
The limit of Riemann sums: ∫ab f(x) dx = limn→∞ Σ f(xᵢ*) · Δx. It represents the net signed area between f and the x-axis over [a, b]. This is the formal definition — the Fundamental Theorem provides a way to compute it via antiderivatives.
Signed Area
The definite integral accumulates positive area where f(x) > 0 and negative area where f(x) < 0. The total is the algebraic sum — regions below the axis subtract from the total.
Convergence Rate
How quickly the error shrinks as n increases. Left/right rules: O(1/n). Midpoint rule: O(1/n²). This means doubling n halves the error for left/right, but quarters it for midpoint.
