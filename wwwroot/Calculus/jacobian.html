§ 01 — The Core Idea

What is the Jacobian, really?

Imagine you have a function F that takes a point in 2D space and maps it to another point in 2D space. For example, polar-to-Cartesian, or a complex transformation that warps a grid. The Jacobian tells you how that function stretches and rotates space locally — at any given point.

Think of it this way: if you zoom in close enough on any differentiable function, it looks like a linear map (a matrix transformation). The Jacobian is that best linear approximation. It's the multivariable generalisation of the ordinary derivative.

"The Jacobian is to a vector-valued function what the derivative is to a scalar function: the best linear approximation at a point."

For a single variable: f(x + h) ≈ f(x) + f′(x)·h. For multiple variables, the scalar multiplier f′(x) becomes a matrix — the Jacobian matrix — and that matrix captures all the directional stretching happening at that point.

§ 02 — The Local Picture

Local Linearity

Differentiability means local linearity. When we say a function F: ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ is differentiable at a point p, we mean there exists a linear map J such that:

F(p + h) = F(p) + J·h + o(‖h‖)
// as ‖h‖ → 0, the error term shrinks faster than h itself

This is exactly the multivariable Taylor expansion to first order. The matrix J is precisely the Jacobian evaluated at p. Every direction you approach from, the function behaves like a linear map — but the amount of stretching and rotating depends on which point you're at.

§ 03 — The Matrix

Building the Jacobian Matrix

Suppose F: ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ maps (x₁, …, xₙ) to (y₁, …, yₘ). Each output component yᵢ is a scalar function of all inputs. The Jacobian matrix arranges all partial derivatives into a grid:

J = ∂(y₁,…,yₘ) / ∂(x₁,…,xₙ) =

⎡ ∂y₁/∂x₁ ∂y₁/∂x₂ ··· ∂y₁/∂xₙ ⎤
⎢ ∂y₂/∂x₁ ∂y₂/∂x₂ ··· ∂y₂/∂xₙ ⎥
⎢ ⋮ ⋮ ⋱ ⋮ ⎥
⎣ ∂yₘ/∂x₁ ∂yₘ/∂x₂ ··· ∂yₘ/∂xₙ ⎦

Each row i is the gradient of the output yᵢ with respect to all inputs. Each column j answers: "if I nudge input xⱼ slightly, how do all the outputs respond?"

Reading the columns as directional derivatives

The j-th column of J is literally DF · eⱼ — the image of the j-th standard basis vector under the linear map. So the columns tell you where the coordinate axes get sent. This is why the Jacobian encodes local geometry so completely.

§ 04 — The Determinant

The Jacobian Determinant: Area as Currency

When the Jacobian is a square matrix (same number of inputs and outputs), its determinant carries a single, beautiful number: the local area (or volume) scaling factor.

|det J| > 1

The function expands area locally. A small patch near this point gets bigger under the map.

|det J| < 1

The function compresses area locally. A small patch shrinks under the map.

det J = 0

The map collapses a dimension. The function is singular here — not locally invertible.

det J < 0

The map reverses orientation — like a reflection. A right-handed region becomes left-handed.

The sign of det(J) tells you whether the transformation preserves or reverses the local orientation of space. The magnitude tells you by how much area scales. Both matter.

§ 05 — The Chain Rule

Composition & the Jacobian Chain Rule

The most important theorem about Jacobians is how they compose. If you chain two maps — G ∘ F — the Jacobian of the composition is the product of the individual Jacobians:

J(G∘F)(x) = JG(F(x)) · JF(x)
// matrix multiplication — order matters!

This is exactly the multivariable chain rule. It says: "the total linear stretching of a composition is the sequential product of the individual linear stretchings." Since matrices multiply, this also means:

det(JG∘F) = det(JG) · det(JF)
// area scaling factors multiply
If F doubles area locally and G triples area locally, then G∘F scales area by 6× locally. The Jacobian determinant is multiplicative under composition — like a "magnification factor" that chains.

Why does this matter for backpropagation?

Neural networks are compositions of functions. The chain rule for Jacobians is literally how gradients flow backward through layers. Each layer's Jacobian participates in the product — which is why deep networks can suffer from vanishing or exploding gradients when those Jacobian magnitudes are consistently less than or greater than 1.

§ 06 — Change of Variables

Integration & Change of Variables

The most practically important use of the Jacobian is in change of variables for integrals. When you substitute u = G(x) in a multivariable integral, the area element dA transforms:

∬R f(x, y) dx dy = ∬S f(G(u,v)) · |det JG(u,v)| du dv
// the |det J| corrects for how area is distorted by the substitution

This is deep: when you "change coordinates", you must account for how the new coordinates stretch or compress area. The Jacobian determinant is precisely that correction factor. Without it, you'd compute the wrong integral.

Polar coordinates: the classic example

The polar map is F(r,θ) = (r cos θ, r sin θ). Its Jacobian determinant is r. That's why:

∬ f(x,y) dx dy = ∬ f(r cos θ, r sin θ) · r dr dθ
// the "r" in polar area elements comes from det(J_polar) = r

Summary: The Jacobian in one glance

As a linear map

J is the best linear approximation of F at a point. It maps velocity vectors in the input to velocity vectors in the output.

As a matrix

J contains all first-order partial derivatives, arranged so that J·h = the first-order change in F when the input shifts by h.

As a determinant

|det J| gives the local volume scaling factor. Its sign encodes orientation. Zero means the map collapses a dimension.

In integration

|det J| appears as the correction factor in change-of-variables formulas, accounting for how coordinates distort area/volume.
